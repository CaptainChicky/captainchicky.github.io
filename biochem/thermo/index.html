Thermodynamics & Protein Structure Math

Gibbs free energy, equilibrium, reaction quotient, Henderson-Hasselbalch, Ramachandran geometry, and helix propensity calculations. Full derivations from first principles.

1. Gibbs Free Energy

The master equation for spontaneity
Definition
The Gibbs free energy change $\Delta G$ tells you whether a process happens spontaneously at constant temperature and pressure:
$$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$$
$\Delta H$ (enthalpy change)
Heat absorbed or released. $\Delta H < 0$ is exothermic (releases heat, favorable).
$\Delta S$ (entropy change)
Change in disorder. $\Delta S > 0$ means more disorder (favorable, since $-T\Delta S$ becomes negative).
Starting point: the second law For any spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe must increase:
$\Delta S_{universe} = \Delta S_{system} + \Delta S_{surroundings} > 0$
At constant pressure, the heat released by the system is absorbed by the surroundings:
$q_{surroundings} = -q_{system} = -\Delta H_{system}$
The entropy change of the surroundings (which acts as a large thermal reservoir at temperature $T$) is:
$\Delta S_{surroundings} = \dfrac{q_{surroundings}}{T} = \dfrac{-\Delta H_{system}}{T}$
Substitute back into the second law:
$\Delta S_{universe} = \Delta S_{system} + \dfrac{-\Delta H_{system}}{T} > 0$
Multiply both sides by $-T$ (this flips the inequality):
$-T\Delta S_{universe} = \Delta H_{system} - T\Delta S_{system} < 0$
Define $\Delta G \equiv \Delta H_{system} - T\Delta S_{system}$. Then the criterion for spontaneity becomes:
$$\Delta G < 0 \implies \text{spontaneous} \qquad \Delta G > 0 \implies \text{non-spontaneous} \qquad \Delta G = 0 \implies \text{equilibrium}$$
Gibbs free energy packages the second law into a single system-only quantity. You no longer need to track the surroundings. $\Delta G$ encodes both the enthalpy preference (are bonds stronger?) and the entropy preference (is disorder increasing?) into one number.

Example: Temperature-dependent spontaneity
A reaction has $\Delta H = -16.1$ kJ/mol and $\Delta S = -21.8$ J/(mol·K). This is the "exothermic but order-increasing" case (row 3). At what temperature does it switch from spontaneous to non-spontaneous?
Set $\Delta G = 0$ and solve for $T$:
$0 = \Delta H - T\Delta S$
$T = \dfrac{\Delta H}{\Delta S} = \dfrac{-16.1\text{ kJ/mol}}{-0.0218\text{ kJ/(mol·K)}} = 738.5\text{ K}$
Watch the unit conversion: $-21.8$ J/(mol·K) $= -0.0218$ kJ/(mol·K). Below 738.5 K: $|T\Delta S|$ is small, enthalpy term dominates, $\Delta G < 0$ (spontaneous). Above 738.5 K: $|T\Delta S|$ grows large, entropy penalty dominates, $\Delta G > 0$ (non-spontaneous).

2. Standard Free Energy & Equilibrium

Connecting $\Delta G°'$ to $K_{eq}$ and the reaction quotient $Q$
Two different $\Delta G$'s
$\Delta G°'$ (standard)
Free energy change when all reactants and products are at their standard-state concentrations (1 M each, pH 7, 25°C). A fixed property of the reaction.
$\Delta G$ (actual)
Free energy change under the actual concentrations in the cell right now. This is what determines direction. Depends on both $\Delta G°'$ and the current concentration ratio.
Setup: Consider the reaction $A \rightleftharpoons B$ The chemical potential of species $A$ at concentration $[A]$ is:
$\mu_A = \mu_A^\circ + RT\ln[A]$
Similarly for $B$:
$\mu_B = \mu_B^\circ + RT\ln[B]$
The free energy change for converting one mole of $A$ into $B$ at these concentrations is:
$\Delta G = \mu_B - \mu_A$
$= (\mu_B^\circ + RT\ln[B]) - (\mu_A^\circ + RT\ln[A])$
$= (\mu_B^\circ - \mu_A^\circ) + RT(\ln[B] - \ln[A])$
The first term $(\mu_B^\circ - \mu_A^\circ)$ is the free energy change when both species are at standard concentration. By definition, this is $\Delta G°'$:
$\Delta G°' = \mu_B^\circ - \mu_A^\circ$
The second term uses the log quotient rule:
$RT(\ln[B] - \ln[A]) = RT\ln\!\left(\dfrac{[B]}{[A]}\right) = RT\ln Q$
where $Q = [B]/[A]$ is the reaction quotient (the mass action ratio at the current concentrations). Combining:
$$\Delta G = \Delta G°' + RT\ln Q$$
For a general reaction $aA + bB \rightleftharpoons cC + dD$, the reaction quotient is $Q = \dfrac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}$. The derivation is identical but uses the stoichiometric sum of chemical potentials.
Now derive $\Delta G°' = -RT\ln K_{eq}$ At equilibrium, $\Delta G = 0$ (no net driving force in either direction). Also, $Q = K_{eq}$ at equilibrium. Substitute both into the master equation:
$0 = \Delta G°' + RT\ln K_{eq}$
Solve for $\Delta G°'$:
$$\Delta G°' = -RT\ln K_{eq}$$
This is not a separate equation. It is the master equation $\Delta G = \Delta G°' + RT\ln Q$ evaluated at the special case of equilibrium ($\Delta G = 0$, $Q = K_{eq}$). Every problem involving $K_{eq}$ and $\Delta G°'$ comes from this one relationship.
Substituting back: the most useful form Replace $\Delta G°'$ with $-RT\ln K_{eq}$ in the master equation:
$\Delta G = -RT\ln K_{eq} + RT\ln Q = RT\ln\!\left(\dfrac{Q}{K_{eq}}\right)$
$$\Delta G = RT\ln\!\left(\frac{Q}{K_{eq}}\right)$$
This form makes the physics crystal clear. If $Q < K_{eq}$: the system has "too much" reactant relative to equilibrium, $\Delta G < 0$, reaction goes forward. If $Q > K_{eq}$: "too much" product, $\Delta G > 0$, reaction goes backward. If $Q = K_{eq}$: equilibrium, $\Delta G = 0$.
Worked examples
Example 1: $\Delta G°'$ from $K_{eq}$
The reaction $A \rightleftharpoons B$ has $K_{eq} = 10$. Find $\Delta G°'$ at 25°C.
$\Delta G°' = -RT\ln K_{eq}$
$= -(8.314 \times 10^{-3})(298)\ln(10)$
$= -(2.478)(2.303)$
$\Delta G°' = -5.7$ kJ/mol
Negative: at standard concentrations the forward reaction is favored, which makes sense since $K_{eq} > 1$ (products favored at equilibrium).
Example 2: Actual $\Delta G$ at cellular concentrations
Same reaction ($K_{eq} = 10$), but now $[A] = 0.9$ mM and $[B] = 0.1$ mM. $T = 37°C = 310$ K.
First find $\Delta G°'$ at 310 K:
$\Delta G°' = -(8.314 \times 10^{-3})(310)\ln(10) = -(2.577)(2.303) = -5.935$ kJ/mol
Compute $Q$:
$Q = \dfrac{[B]}{[A]} = \dfrac{0.1}{0.9} = 0.111$
Apply the master equation:
$\Delta G = \Delta G°' + RT\ln Q$
$= -5.935 + (2.577)\ln(0.111)$
$= -5.935 + (2.577)(-2.198)$
$= -5.935 + (-5.664)$
$\Delta G = -11.6$ kJ/mol
Even more negative than $\Delta G°'$! The low $Q$ (mostly reactant $A$) means the system is far from equilibrium, strongly favoring $A \to B$.
Key point: $\Delta G°'$ is a fixed property of the reaction (only depends on $K_{eq}$ and $T$). $\Delta G$ is a snapshot of the current state (depends on actual concentrations via $Q$). A reaction with a positive $\Delta G°'$ can still proceed forward if the concentrations are far enough from equilibrium that $RT\ln Q$ is sufficiently negative.

3. Henderson-Hasselbalch & Net Charge

Predicting protonation states and peptide charge from pH and pKa
The acid dissociation equilibrium
A weak acid $HA$ dissociates in water: $$HA \rightleftharpoons A^- + H^+$$
The equilibrium expression is:
$K_a = \dfrac{[A^-][H^+]}{[HA]}$
Start from the $K_a$ expression:
$K_a = \dfrac{[A^-][H^+]}{[HA]}$
Take the negative $\log_{10}$ of both sides:
$-\log K_a = -\log\!\left(\dfrac{[A^-][H^+]}{[HA]}\right)$
The left side is $pK_a$ by definition. Expand the right side using log rules:
$pK_a = -\log[H^+] - \log\!\left(\dfrac{[A^-]}{[HA]}\right)$
Recognize that $-\log[H^+] = pH$:
$pK_a = pH - \log\!\left(\dfrac{[A^-]}{[HA]}\right)$
Rearrange to solve for $pH$:
$$pH = pK_a + \log\!\left(\frac{[A^-]}{[HA]}\right)$$
When $pH = pK_a$: the log term is 0, so $[A^-] = [HA]$. Exactly half is protonated. When $pH > pK_a$ (by 1 or more units): the deprotonated form $A^-$ dominates. When $pH < pK_a$ (by 1 or more units): the protonated form $HA$ dominates.
Deriving the fraction protonated From $K_a = [A^-][H^+]/[HA]$, solve for the ratio:
$\dfrac{[A^-]}{[HA]} = \dfrac{K_a}{[H^+]} = 10^{pH - pK_a}$
Let $f_{HA}$ be the fraction protonated and $f_{A^-}$ the fraction deprotonated. Since $f_{HA} + f_{A^-} = 1$:
$f_{A^-} = \dfrac{[A^-]}{[HA] + [A^-]} = \dfrac{10^{pH - pK_a}}{1 + 10^{pH - pK_a}}$
$f_{HA} = \dfrac{1}{1 + 10^{pH - pK_a}}$
These fractions let you compute the exact charge contribution from any ionizable group at any pH, not just the "dominant species" shortcut. For exam-level problems where $pH$ is far from $pK_a$ (more than ~2 units away), the dominant-species shortcut works fine.
The shortcut rule for charge states
For acidic groups (COOH, Asp, Glu, Cys, Tyr): Protonated form = neutral ($HA$). Deprotonated form = negative ($A^-$).
$pH < pK_a$: protonated → charge $= 0$
$pH > pK_a$: deprotonated → charge $= -1$
For basic groups (NH₃⁺ at N-terminus, Lys, Arg, His): Protonated form = positive ($BH^+$). Deprotonated form = neutral ($B$).
$pH < pK_a$: protonated → charge $= +1$
$pH > pK_a$: deprotonated → charge $= 0$

4. Ramachandran Geometry

Backbone torsion angles and allowed conformations
Defining $\phi$ and $\psi$
The peptide bond ($C$-$N$) is planar and rigid (partial double-bond character, no free rotation). But the bonds flanking each $C_\alpha$ can rotate. These two rotations define the backbone conformation of each residue:
$\phi$ (phi)
Rotation about the $N$-$C_\alpha$ bond. Defined as the dihedral angle $C_{i-1}$-$N$-$C_\alpha$-$C$.
$\psi$ (psi)
Rotation about the $C_\alpha$-$C$ bond. Defined as the dihedral angle $N$-$C_\alpha$-$C$-$N_{i+1}$.
Both angles range from $-180°$ to $+180°$. Most combinations cause steric clashes between backbone atoms or between the backbone and the side chain. A Ramachandran plot maps $\phi$ (x-axis) vs. $\psi$ (y-axis) and highlights the sterically allowed regions.
The steric clash argument Each atom has a van der Waals radius. If two non-bonded atoms are forced closer than the sum of their radii, the potential energy spikes (Lennard-Jones repulsion). The calculation is:
$d_{ij}(\phi, \psi) < r_i^{vdW} + r_j^{vdW} \implies$ steric clash $\implies$ that $(\phi, \psi)$ is forbidden
For each $(\phi, \psi)$ pair, compute the 3D coordinates of every backbone (and $C_\beta$) atom using the rigid peptide plane geometry + the two torsion angles. Then check all pairwise distances. Allowed regions for most amino acids:
$\alpha$-helix: $\phi \approx -57°$, $\psi \approx -47°$
$\beta$-sheet: $\phi \approx -120°$ to $-140°$, $\psi \approx +110°$ to $+135°$
Left-handed helix: $\phi \approx +60°$, $\psi \approx +45°$ (rare, sterically strained for L-amino acids)
Why glycine is special: Glycine has no side chain (R = H). The $C_\beta$ atom causes most of the steric clashes in other amino acids. Without it, glycine can access $\phi$/$\psi$ regions that are forbidden for everything else (nearly the entire plot is allowed). Why proline is special: Proline's side chain is covalently bonded back to the backbone nitrogen, forming a five-membered ring. This locks $\phi$ to approximately $-63°$ (very little rotational freedom). Proline is confined to a narrow vertical strip on the Ramachandran plot and is incompatible with the interior of an $\alpha$-helix (it lacks the backbone $N$-$H$ needed for the $i \to i+4$ hydrogen bond).
$\alpha$-helix geometry
Key parameters:
Residues per turn: $3.6$
Rise per residue: $1.5$ Å → Pitch (rise per turn): $3.6 \times 1.5 = 5.4$ Å
Hydrogen bond pattern: $C{=}O$ of residue $i$ bonds to $N{-}H$ of residue $i+4$
$\phi = -57°$, $\psi = -47°$ (right-handed helix)
The $i$ to $i+4$ spacing means that for a peptide segment like residues 1-2-3-4-5-6-7, the $C{=}O$ of residue 3 forms a hydrogen bond with the $N{-}H$ of residue 7 (three residues ahead in sequence, four peptide bonds apart).

5. Helix Propensity ($\Delta\Delta G°$)

Predicting $\alpha$-helix stability from sequence
What $\Delta\Delta G°$ measures
$\Delta\Delta G°$ for a given amino acid is the additional free energy cost (relative to alanine) required for that residue to adopt the $\alpha$-helical conformation. Alanine is the reference ($\Delta\Delta G° = 0$) because it has the highest intrinsic helix propensity among the common amino acids.
Formal definition: For each amino acid $X$, measure the equilibrium between coil and helix for a host peptide with $X$ at a guest site:
$K_{helix}^X = \dfrac{[\text{helix}]}{[\text{coil}]}$ for amino acid $X$
$\Delta G°_{helix}^X = -RT\ln K_{helix}^X$
The $\Delta\Delta G°$ is defined relative to alanine:
$\Delta\Delta G°_X = \Delta G°_{helix}^X - \Delta G°_{helix}^{Ala}$
$= -RT\ln K_{helix}^X - (-RT\ln K_{helix}^{Ala})$
$= -RT\ln\!\left(\dfrac{K_{helix}^X}{K_{helix}^{Ala}}\right)$
If $\Delta\Delta G° > 0$: residue $X$ is worse at forming helix than Ala (less favorable). If $\Delta\Delta G° = 0$: equivalent to Ala (strong helix former). Comparing two peptides: To estimate which peptide is more likely to form a helix, sum the $\Delta\Delta G°$ values for all residues:
$\Sigma\Delta\Delta G°_{peptide} = \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} \Delta\Delta G°_{residue\,i}$
Lower total = easier to form helix = more likely to be helical.

Example: Comparing two peptides
Peptide A: LKAENDEAARAMSEA. Peptide B: CRAGGFPWDQPGTSN.
Peptide A is rich in Ala ($\Delta\Delta G° = 0$), Leu ($0.6$), Glu ($1.3$), with no Gly or Pro. Peptide B contains two Pro ($13.0$ each) and three Gly ($4.6$ each), which alone contribute $2(13.0) + 3(4.6) = 39.8$ kJ/mol. Peptide A has a much lower total $\Sigma\Delta\Delta G°$, so it is far more likely to form an $\alpha$-helix. This matches intuition: Pro is a helix breaker (rigid ring, no N-H donor) and Gly is too conformationally flexible to maintain the precise $\phi/\psi$ angles required.
