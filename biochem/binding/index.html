1. Ligand Binding Fundamentals

Reversible binding, dissociation constant, and fractional saturation
The binding equilibrium
A protein $P$ binds a ligand $L$ reversibly to form the complex $PL$:
$$P + L \underset{k_{off}}{\overset{k_{on}}{\rightleftharpoons}} PL$$
Association constant
$K_a = \dfrac{[PL]}{[P][L]}$
Measures affinity. Larger $K_a$ = tighter binding.
Dissociation constant
$K_d = \dfrac{[P][L]}{[PL]} = \dfrac{1}{K_a}$
Has units of concentration. Smaller $K_d$ = tighter binding. Typical biological $K_d$ values range from picomolar to millimolar.
Fractional saturation ($\theta$)
$\theta$ is the fraction of binding sites that are occupied: $\quad \theta = \dfrac{[PL]}{[PL] + [P]} = \dfrac{\text{occupied sites}}{\text{total sites}}$
$\theta$ ranges from 0 (nothing bound) to 1 (fully saturated). For oxygen-binding proteins, $\theta$ is often written as $Y_{O_2}$.
Start from the definition of $\theta$:
$\theta = \dfrac{[PL]}{[PL] + [P]}$
From the equilibrium, solve for $[PL]$:
$K_d = \dfrac{[P][L]}{[PL]} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad [PL] = \dfrac{[P][L]}{K_d}$
Substitute into the $\theta$ expression:
$\theta = \dfrac{[P][L]/K_d}{[P][L]/K_d + [P]}$
Factor out $[P]$ from numerator and denominator (it cancels):
$\theta = \dfrac{[L]/K_d}{[L]/K_d + 1} = \dfrac{[L]}{[L] + K_d}$
Result:
$$\theta = \frac{[L]}{[L] + K_d}$$
Physical meaning of $K_d$: Set $\theta = 0.5$ (half-saturation). Then $0.5 = [L]/([L] + K_d)$, which gives $[L] = K_d$. So $K_d$ is the ligand concentration at which half the binding sites are occupied. For oxygen binding proteins, this is called the $P_{50}$: the partial pressure of $O_2$ at half-saturation.

2. Myoglobin vs. Hemoglobin Binding

Hyperbolic vs. sigmoidal curves and why it matters for O₂ transport
Myoglobin (hyperbolic)
Single subunit, one O₂ binding site. Follows the simple binding equation: $\theta = \dfrac{pO_2}{pO_2 + P_{50}}$
Very high affinity ($P_{50} \approx 0.3$ kPa). Binds O₂ tightly at almost any physiological $pO_2$. Great for storing O₂ in muscle, but terrible for transporting it, because it barely releases O₂ even when tissue $pO_2$ drops.
Hemoglobin (sigmoidal)
Four subunits ($\alpha_2\beta_2$), four O₂ binding sites. Displays cooperative binding: $\theta = \dfrac{[L]^{n_H}}{[L]^{n_H} + K_d^{n_H}}$
Moderate affinity ($P_{50} \approx 3.5$ kPa). The sigmoidal shape means Hb loads efficiently in the lungs ($pO_2 \approx 13$ kPa) and unloads efficiently in tissues ($pO_2 \approx 4$ kPa). The steep middle section is the "functional range" where small $pO_2$ changes cause large $\theta$ changes.
Why myoglobin can't transport: At lung $pO_2$ (13 kPa) Mb is ~98% saturated. At resting tissue $pO_2$ (4 kPa) it's still ~93% saturated. That's only ~5% release. Hemoglobin goes from ~98% to ~70%, releasing ~28% of its O₂, more than five times as much.

3. Cooperativity & T/R State Transition

How binding at one subunit influences the others
Two-state model
Hemoglobin exists in equilibrium between two quaternary conformations:
T-state (tense)
Low O₂ affinity. Stabilized by ionic interactions (salt bridges) at subunit interfaces. Predominant when deoxy.
R-state (relaxed)
High O₂ affinity. Subunit interfaces rearranged. Predominant when oxygenated.
Mechanism of cooperativity
When O₂ binds to a heme iron:
1. $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ moves into the plane of the porphyrin ring 2. The proximal histidine (His F8) is pulled with it 3. The F-helix shifts position 4. Non-covalent contacts at subunit interfaces rearrange 5. Neighboring subunits are nudged toward the R-state, increasing their O₂ affinity
The result: the first O₂ binds with the lowest affinity, and each subsequent O₂ binds more easily. The last O₂ binds with roughly 100x higher affinity than the first. This is positive cooperativity.

4. The Hill Equation & Hill Plots

Quantifying cooperativity
Setup
For a protein with $n$ identical binding sites that all fill simultaneously (a simplification), the binding equilibrium is: $$P + nL \rightleftharpoons PL_n$$
Write the equilibrium expression for the "all-or-nothing" binding model:
$K_d = \dfrac{[P][L]^n}{[PL_n]} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad [PL_n] = \dfrac{[P][L]^n}{K_d}$
Fractional saturation (only two species: free $P$ and fully-bound $PL_n$):
$\theta = \dfrac{[PL_n]}{[P] + [PL_n]}$
Substitute in $[PL_n]$:
$\theta = \dfrac{[P][L]^n / K_d}{[P] + [P][L]^n / K_d}$
Cancel $[P]$:
$\theta = \dfrac{[L]^n / K_d}{1 + [L]^n / K_d} = \dfrac{[L]^n}{[L]^n + K_d}$
Hill equation:
$$\theta = \frac{[L]^{n_H}}{[L]^{n_H} + K_d^{n_H}}$$
We replace $n$ with $n_H$ (the Hill coefficient) because real proteins don't have truly all-or-nothing binding. $n_H$ is an empirical measure of cooperativity, not the literal number of binding sites. Equivalently, $K_d$ can be written as $K_{0.5}^{n_H}$ where $K_{0.5}$ (or $P_{50}$) is the ligand concentration at half-saturation.
Now rearrange to get the Hill plot form. Start from:
$\theta = \dfrac{[L]^{n_H}}{[L]^{n_H} + K_d^{n_H}}$
Compute $\dfrac{\theta}{1 - \theta}$:
$1 - \theta = \dfrac{K_d^{n_H}}{[L]^{n_H} + K_d^{n_H}}$
$\dfrac{\theta}{1-\theta} = \dfrac{[L]^{n_H}}{K_d^{n_H}}$
Take $\log$ of both sides:
$\log\!\left(\dfrac{\theta}{1-\theta}\right) = n_H \log[L] - n_H \log K_d$
Hill plot equation ($y = mx + b$):
$$\log\!\left(\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}\right) = n_H \log[L] - n_H \log K_{0.5}$$
Plot $\log(\theta/(1-\theta))$ on the y-axis vs. $\log[L]$ on the x-axis. The slope in the transition region (around $\theta = 0.5$) gives $n_H$. When $\theta = 0.5$, $\log(\theta/(1-\theta)) = 0$, so the x-intercept gives $\log K_{0.5}$ (which is $\log P_{50}$ for oxygen).
Interpreting the Hill coefficient
$n_H$MeaningCurve shape
$n_H = 1$No cooperativity (independent sites)Hyperbolic (like myoglobin)
$n_H > 1$Positive cooperativity (binding helps binding)Sigmoidal (like hemoglobin, $n_H \approx 2.8$)
$n_H < 1$Negative cooperativity (binding hinders binding)Flatter than hyperbolic
Important: $n_H$ is always $\leq n$ (the actual number of binding sites). Hemoglobin has 4 binding sites but $n_H \approx 2.8$, because binding is cooperative but not perfectly all-or-nothing. $n_H = n$ would mean infinite cooperativity.

5. Allosteric Effectors of Hemoglobin

How temperature, pH, CO₂, and 2,3-BPG shift the O₂ binding curve
All four effectors below stabilize the T-state and are negative heterotropic allosteric effectors. They right-shift the O₂ binding curve (increase $P_{50}$, decrease affinity), promoting O₂ release to tissues.
EffectorChangeEffect on HbMechanism
Temperature ↑ Right shift (↓ affinity) Higher thermal energy weakens non-covalent interactions that stabilize the R-state. T-state becomes favored.
H⁺ (pH) ↓ pH (more H⁺) Right shift (↓ affinity) Protons bind to His residues at subunit interfaces, forming additional salt bridges that stabilize the T-state. This is the Bohr effect.
CO₂ ↑ Right shift (↓ affinity) Two mechanisms: (1) CO₂ reacts with N-termini to form carbamate groups, adding ionic interactions that stabilize T-state. (2) CO₂ + H₂O → HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ (via carbonic anhydrase), contributing to the Bohr effect.
2,3-BPG ↑ Right shift (↓ affinity) 2,3-BPG (a glycolysis byproduct) binds in the central cavity between β subunits, forming cross-links that stabilize the T-state. The cavity is only large enough in the T conformation.
Putting it together: Rapidly metabolizing tissues release heat, produce CO₂, generate acid, and make 2,3-BPG. All four simultaneously right-shift the Hb curve, forcing O₂ release exactly where it's needed most. In the lungs, the reverse conditions (cool air, low CO₂, higher pH) left-shift the curve back, promoting O₂ loading.
Special case: Fetal hemoglobin ($\alpha_2\gamma_2$)
Fetal Hb (HbF) has $\gamma$ subunits instead of $\beta$. The key mutation is H143S in the $\gamma$ chain: serine cannot form the same ionic interaction with 2,3-BPG that histidine can. Result: HbF binds 2,3-BPG more weakly, so it has higher O₂ affinity than adult HbA. This lets the fetus pull O₂ from maternal blood across the placenta.

6. Energetics of Membrane Transport

Free energy of moving solutes across a membrane
The thermodynamic question
Moving a solute from concentration $C_1$ (starting side) to $C_2$ (destination side) across a membrane: what is $\Delta G$?
Uncharged solute
$$\Delta G = RT\ln\!\left(\frac{C_2}{C_1}\right)$$
Charged solute (ion)
$$\Delta G = RT\ln\!\left(\frac{C_2}{C_1}\right) + ZF\Delta V$$
Part A: Uncharged solute The chemical potential of a solute at concentration $C$ is:
$\mu = \mu^\circ + RT\ln C$
where $\mu^\circ$ is the standard chemical potential, $R = 8.314 \times 10^{-3}$ kJ/(mol·K), and $T$ is temperature in Kelvin. The free energy change for moving one mole from side 1 ($C_1$) to side 2 ($C_2$) is:
$\Delta G = \mu_2 - \mu_1 = (\mu^\circ + RT\ln C_2) - (\mu^\circ + RT\ln C_1)$
The $\mu^\circ$ terms cancel (same solute, same standard state):
$\Delta G = RT\ln C_2 - RT\ln C_1 = RT\ln\!\left(\dfrac{C_2}{C_1}\right)$
If $C_2 < C_1$ (moving down the gradient): $\ln(C_2/C_1) < 0$, so $\Delta G < 0$ (spontaneous). This is passive transport. If $C_2 > C_1$ (moving against the gradient): $\ln(C_2/C_1) > 0$, so $\Delta G > 0$ (not spontaneous). This is active transport.
Part B: Charged solute (ion) For an ion, the chemical potential includes an electrical work term. The electrochemical potential is:
$\tilde{\mu} = \mu^\circ + RT\ln C + ZF\psi$
where $Z$ = ion charge, $F = 96.5$ kJ/(V·mol) (Faraday's constant), and $\psi$ = electrical potential at that location. The free energy change is:
$\Delta G = \tilde{\mu}_2 - \tilde{\mu}_1$
$= (\mu^\circ + RT\ln C_2 + ZF\psi_2) - (\mu^\circ + RT\ln C_1 + ZF\psi_1)$
Simplify ($\mu^\circ$ cancels, define $\Delta V = \psi_2 - \psi_1$):
$\Delta G = RT\ln\!\left(\dfrac{C_2}{C_1}\right) + ZF\Delta V$
The first term is the concentration gradient contribution. The second term is the electrical gradient (membrane potential) contribution. Together they form the electrochemical gradient.
Sign convention: $C_1$ = starting concentration, $C_2$ = destination concentration. $\Delta V$ = potential at destination minus potential at start. For a cell with interior at $-60$ mV relative to outside, if you're transporting from outside to inside: $\Delta V = -60\text{ mV} = -0.060\text{ V}$.

Worked examples
Example 1: Uncharged solute, against gradient
Transport an uncharged molecule from 5 mM outside to 150 mM inside at 25°C (298 K).
$\Delta G = RT\ln\!\left(\dfrac{C_2}{C_1}\right) = (8.314 \times 10^{-3})(298)\ln\!\left(\dfrac{150}{5}\right)$
$= 2.478 \times \ln(30) = 2.478 \times 3.401$
$\Delta G = +8.43$ kJ/mol
$\Delta G > 0$: this is active transport (requires energy input).
Example 2: Cation (+1) across membrane potential
Same concentrations (5 mM → 150 mM), but now the solute is an ion with $Z = +1$ and $\Delta V = -0.060$ V (interior negative). $T = 298$ K.
Concentration term (same as before):
$RT\ln\!\left(\dfrac{C_2}{C_1}\right) = +8.43\text{ kJ/mol}$
Electrical term:
$ZF\Delta V = (+1)(96.5)(-0.060) = -5.79\text{ kJ/mol}$
Total:
$\Delta G = 8.43 + (-5.79) = +2.64$ kJ/mol
Still positive (active transport), but the membrane potential partially favors entry of the cation, making it less costly.
Example 3: What if the ion were $Z = -1$ instead?
The concentration term is unchanged: $+8.43$ kJ/mol Electrical term flips sign:
$ZF\Delta V = (-1)(96.5)(-0.060) = +5.79\text{ kJ/mol}$
$\Delta G = 8.43 + 5.79 = +14.22$ kJ/mol
Much less favorable. The negative charge is being pushed away from the negative interior. Both the concentration gradient and the electrical gradient oppose this transport.
Decision rule: $\Delta G < 0$ → passive (spontaneous, no energy needed). $\Delta G > 0$ → active (requires energy input, e.g. ATP hydrolysis). The sign of $ZF\Delta V$ can either help or hurt, depending on whether the ion charge and membrane potential are working together or against each other.
